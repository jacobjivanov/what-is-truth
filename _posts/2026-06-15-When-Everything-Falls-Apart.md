Tonight, this message will be based on a book that is not frequently preached about, talked about, or read. This year, one of my main New Year's Resolutions was to become more familiar with the Old Testament prophets. It was the section of Scripture that I was least familiar with, and so in my plan, I recently studied Jeremiah. The books of Jeremiah and Lamentations are very raw, and at several points I was startled by what I read. 

Tonight, I would like to read to you a passage from the book of Lamentations. I believe it is important, especially for areas of Scripture we are less familiar with, to read passages with a heavy dose of context, or we risk slightly or completely misinterpreting the point. I was originally planning to read the entirety of the book, five chapters (it takes 17 minutes), but on the advice of someone older and wiser than I, it will be only the third chapter.

To many of you, this passage is unfamiliar. But how would you describe the tone? What feeling would it evoke? The book of Lamentations is extremely somber, dismal, maybe a little depressing. So why did I read it? 

**2 Timothy 3:16-17** ESV
> All Scripture is breathed out by God and profitable for teaching, for reproof, for correction, and for training in righteousness, that the man of God may be complete, equipped for every good work.

Since all Scripture is breathed out by God, it should all be diligently studied. We should not avoid areas of Scripture because they aren't as clear to us, or make us feel uncomfortable. So let's try to understand what is going on.

While the author of Lamentations does not identify themselves, the context of the book, the specific events described in it, and the phrases used in the book point to Jeremiah as being the author. There are also a few allusions to specific events in Jeremiah's life.

Jeremiah lived in the final decades of the Southern Kingdom of Judah, several hundred years after King David, and about a hundred years after the Northern Kingdom of Israel had been conquered by the Assyrian Empire. And when he was a young man, God called him and commissioned him to be a prophet on His behalf. And Jeremiah had the depressing task of desperately preaching to the kings, priests, and people of Judah to repent and serve God, or else God would allow the Babylonian Empire to conquer their nation. Over and over, Jeremiah went to the temple and preached this message of imminent disaster, and even though he was continuously proven right, he was ignored and imprisoned. On several instances, the king would ask Jeremiah what he should do, would listen, and then wouldn't do it. Almost no one respected him, and he was constantly alone except for one friend named Baruch, who would speak on his behalf when he was banned from the temple.

But after four decades of warnings, in 586 BC God allowed the Babylonian Empire to conquer Judah, burn it to the ground, and enslave its citizens. And Jeremiah, looking around at the ruins of his city, and the people who refused to listen to him and serve God, wrote Lamentations as an emotional response to this catastrophe.

How? Why? These are the questions that Jeremiah was grappling with throughout his entire life. 

Why did God allow Israel and Judah to fall? When God gave Moses the Law, like a contract, there were stipulations. If Israel obeyed the Law, God would protect and bless them, but if Israel disobeyed the Law, God would bring disaster upon them and would remove them from their land and would scatter them. And over the next 800 years, God constantly showed mercy to Israel. Even though they continuously rebelled against God, God would send judges, or good kings, or prophets who would restore Israel to God. But with each generation, it just got worse and worse.

How bad did it get? By the time Jeremiah lived, the people of Israel would go to the temple to sacrifice to God, and then would walk down the street to the Valley of Hinnom outside of Jerusalem, and sacrifice their own children to pagan gods. And prophets would beg desperately for the people of Israel to stop, but they were just ignored. 

So God allowed the Babylonian Empire to crush Judah.

Later, when the King of Judah attempts to defend Jerusalem, God is even more specific

**Jeremiah 21:8-10** ESV
> “And to this people you shall say: ‘Thus says the LORD: Behold, I set before you the way of life and the way of death. He who stays in this city shall die by the sword, by famine, and by pestilence, but he who goes out and surrenders to the Chaldeans who are besieging you shall live and shall have his life as a prize of war. For I have set my face against this city for harm and not for good, declares the LORD: it shall be given into the hand of the king of Babylon, and he shall burn it with fire.’

The startling phrase is that God plans 'harm and not good' against Jerusalem. And this phrase is repeated throughout the book. Those that try to defend Jerusalem, God will allow them to be conquered. Those that try to escape to Egypt or Assyria, God will allow them to be conquered.

But God, even in the midst of His righteous judgement against Israel, still has mercy. Repeatedly, God says that those that surrender to the Babylonian Empire would survive. In contrast to God's plan for harm against the rebels in Jerusalem, God promises blessing to them.

**Jeremiah 29:4-14** ESV

Take wives and have sons and daughters; take wives for your sons, and give your daughters in marriage, that they may bear sons and daughters; multiply there, and do not decrease. For I know the plans I have for you, declares the LORD, plans for welfare and not for evil, to give you a future and a hope. Then you will call upon me and come and pray to me, and I will hear you. You will seek me and find me, when you seek me with all your heart. I will be found by you, declares the LORD, and I will restore your fortunes and gather you from all the nations and all the places where I have driven you, declares the LORD, and I will bring you back to the place from which I sent you into exile.

And after 70 years, just like God promised, the Persian Empire conquered the Babylonian Empire and let the Israelites captives return to their land. God raised up leaders like Daniel to protect them, when they returned, leaders like Ezra and Nehemiah, and God providentially protected them through Esther and Mordecai.

God would not let the rebellion of Israel stop His sovereign plan. God promised that a descendent of Adam would crush the serpent. God promised that a descendent of Abraham would bring blessing to all nations. God promised that a descendent of David would rule on the throne forever. God promised that a suffering servant would bear the weight of humanities sin. God promised that after Babylon, Persia, Greece, and Rome had their time to rule, that God would set up a Kingdom that would never be destroyed. 

You see there was a problem. The Old Testament Law exposed humanities sinful nature. It showed us how tragically depraved our natural condition is. It showed us that we desperately needed a savior, not from some invading kingdom or external threat, but from our own self.

And the prophets of the Old Testament saw glimpses of how this would happen.

**Ezekiel 36:24-28** ESV

I will sprinkle clean water on you, and you shall be clean from all your uncleannesses, and from all your idols I will cleanse you. And I will give you a new heart, and a new spirit I will put within you. And I will remove the heart of stone from your flesh and give you a heart of flesh. And these promises all came true. Jesus Christ, 500 years after Jeremiah, was born of the virgin Mary, lived a sinless life, was crucified under Pontius Pilate, died and was buried, and three days later He rose again. On Pentecost, the early church received the Holy Spirit, who lives in every person who has put their faith in Christ to this day.

How can we understand and apply the book of Lamentations?
- We can trust that God is sovereign and has control over history
- We should be reverently aware of the just consequences of disobeying God's Law and simultaneously we should be immensely grateful that our salvation, our righteousness, is not from obedience to this impossible Law, but out of the sheer grace of God
- In situations where it feels like our world is falling apart, we can have some perspective and say that if Jeremiah was able to trust God, we can too.

Jeremiah trusted in the eschatological promise of his day, that God would restore his people, return them to their land, and send them a savior who would instantiate a new covenant. Now, we look back in history at the life of Christ, and we know that He will come again.
